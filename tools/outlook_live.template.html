Outlook (live, experimental): where very strong ocean storms may occur in the next 4 days

Experimental. Not a warning. This page turns the latest computer-model forecasts into a map of where very strong winds over the North Atlantic and North Pacific may occur in the next four days, and shows how often forecasts like these matched what OPC (NOAA’s Ocean Prediction Center) called in the past. For warnings use OPC’s own products.

What you are looking at

Blue shading shows where, in past cases with a forecast like this one, OPC had a strong low nearby. The stronger the blue, the more often that happened:

  • Light blue: about 1 to 3 times in 10.
  • Medium blue: about 3 to 5 times in 10.
  • Dark blue: 5 times in 10 or more.
  • No shading: under 1 in 10. That is a lower chance, not a guarantee of calm.

How to use it: choose a day (Day 1 is the first 24 hours of the forecast), a basin and a strength, then hover or tap the map. The lookup card gives the number for that spot.

“OPC agreed” means OPC’s own analysis had a low of that strength within 324 nautical miles of the point on that day. Hurricane force is wind of 64 kt or more, storm force 48 to 63 kt.

What you are looking at

Blue shading shows the forecast wind, a weighted average of GFS, ECMWF and AIFS, with extra wind added where the ensemble runs spread out:

  • Light blue: wind of 34 to 47 kt (gale force).
  • Medium blue: 48 to 63 kt (storm force).
  • Dark blue: 64 kt or more (hurricane force).
  • No shading: under 34 kt.

This is sustained wind, the kind OPC warns on, not gusts. Hover or tap the map: the card shows the wind from each model and the chance of each wind level, worked out from about 130 ensemble runs. Those chances mean “this wind occurs” and were checked against buoys and ships on the GFE wind page. They are not the “OPC agreed” rates of the first layer, and no OPC rates have been measured for this layer.

Other marks on the map (context only)

These come from the same GFS forecast, at the end of the day shown. They help you place the shading; they do not change it and have no percentages.

  • L: a low-pressure centre of 992 hPa or lower, with its pressure. Lows over high ground such as Greenland are left out, because their sea-level pressure is not real.
  • Orange dashed line: a dip (trough) in the winds about 5.5 km up (the 500 hPa level). It is a simple finder and is often the west side of a storm’s own upper-level low.
  • Purple: the core of the jet stream (winds of 110 kt or more at about 10 km, the 250 hPa level).
  • Hatched circle (about 500 km): a low with strong upper-level lift (500 hPa vorticity advection) over a strong temperature contrast. In past cases lows in this setup were more likely to reach storm force within a day. A hint only, not a forecast; details in “How this works”.

Keep in mind

  • This is a computer forecast, not a warning.
  • The first layer uses the model’s forecast gusts as a stand-in for the sustained wind OPC warns on, and OPC’s hurricane-force call is an analyst’s judgement. So “OPC agreed” does not mean the wind was that strong.
  • No shading does not mean safe. It means that in the past, forecasts like this were followed by an OPC low less than 1 time in 10.
  • The percentages come from five past seasons of one model, so read them as rough rates, not exact odds. Distances are in nautical miles.
  • The wind-blend layer has no OPC rates behind it.
  • Around Greenland the wind-blend layer may under-show the strongest winds. In one winter (2025-26) of satellite winds over the North Atlantic, reported by the marine wind project and not checked here, where the blend showed gale it showed storm force (48 kt or more) in only 0 to 3 cases in 100. Satellite winds reached storm force in about 2 in 10 of those gale cells in northerly flow along the Greenland east coast and in cold-air easterly flow, among the largest gaps. Read the blue shading there as a floor, not a ceiling. This was not tested elsewhere.
  • Only forecast days 1 to 4 and only October to April. The first layer has no gale shading, because there is no OPC record of gale-force lows to measure against.
How this works (details for the curious)

The first layer: how often OPC agreed

For each point on a 1-degree ocean grid we take the strongest GFS forecast gust within a set distance on that forecast day (60 nautical miles on Day 1, 120 on Day 2, 270 on Days 3 and 4). These distances allow for the model putting a low in the wrong place: they are the smallest that still caught at least 80% of the hurricane-force gust area when tested against ERA5, a model-based record of past weather, not observations. We then look up how often, in five past seasons (October–April 2021–22 to 2025–26) of 00 UTC GFS runs, OPC had a hurricane-force or storm-force low fix within 324 nautical miles of points with that gust. Those shares are the percentages. The areas are drawn from the model’s own wind field, not from tracking a low, so a storm the tracker misses still shows.

The second layer: the three-model blend

It is the recipe behind the GFE wind page. GFS, ECMWF and AIFS 10 m sustained wind are averaged, each weighted by how many of about 80 GEFS and ECMWF ensemble runs (the same model run many times from slightly different starting points) are close to it, and a quarter of the spread between runs (0.15 on Day 1), up to 5 kt, is added; this puts back the strong winds that averaging flattens. The chances come from about 130 ensemble runs (GEFS, the ECMWF ensemble and the AIFS ensemble) and were adjusted on the GFE page to match buoys and ships over past cases from March 2025 to March 2026. Each day shows the higher of its two 12-hourly forecast times (+12 and +24 h for Day 1), a little more area than the single-time test the recipe was tuned on, and the highest value in each 1-degree cell. On the one run checked (14 October 00 UTC, North Atlantic, +48 h), GFS had about four times ECMWF’s number of cells at 34 kt or more, and the blend sits between them. The blend is built from the same 00 UTC run as the first layer but is added later (the ECMWF ensemble is out about 8 hours after the run); until it is ready the layer says so.

The marks on the map

Lows are GFS pressure minima of 992 hPa or lower; the shading never uses them. The jet core is 250 hPa wind of 110 kt or more. The hatch marks a low with both strong 500 hPa vorticity advection (upper-level lift) and a strong low-level temperature contrast (baroclinic zone). “Strong” means in the top third of past ERA5 cyclone positions from 2004–2026, a cut-off chosen after looking at the data and not tested on GFS. Among those past ERA5 lows, the next 24 hours reached storm force or stronger 41% of the time when both were present against 8% when neither was. That is a different set of lows from the ones drawn here, so read it as a hint, not a rate for this map.

Limits

The percentages come from five past seasons of 00 UTC runs of one model, and the model was upgraded during that time, so read them as rough rates, not precise odds. Gust is sampled every 6 hours, so a short peak can be missed. Neighbouring points in one run are not separate tests, so the five seasons are what limit how sure we can be. The tables here use all five seasons; the replay page leaves out the season being shown. The shading distances come from a test against ERA5 (a model-based record, not observations). Land and coasts are not scored. Nothing beyond day 4, nothing from May to September, and the map is rebuilt once a day, so it can be up to a day behind the latest GFS run.
